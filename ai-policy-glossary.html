How to read the guide

Definitions are choices, not neutral facts.

Technical terms describe how systems are built or behave. Legal categories apply only within a specific rule or jurisdiction. Informal terms can be useful in debate but may hide contested assumptions.

This guide uses plain-language synthesis rather than reproducing legal text. For binding decisions, consult the underlying law, standard, or agency guidance.

01 · System basics

How AI systems work

The building blocks behind model development and deployment. These terms help distinguish a trained model from the larger system, infrastructure, and human process around it.

01

Policy definition

Artificial intelligence (AI)

A machine-based system that infers how to generate outputs—such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions—from inputs and objectives. Systems vary in autonomy and whether they adapt after deployment. OECD basis

Policy lensRegulation usually defines an “AI system,” not intelligence itself. Scope depends on the exact legal definition and exemptions.

02

Core term

Machine learning (ML)

Techniques that improve performance by learning patterns from data rather than relying only on hand-written rules. Training produces or adjusts a model that can be used on new inputs.

Policy lensLearning from data shifts attention toward data quality, representativeness, rights, documentation, and post-deployment monitoring.

03

Core term

Deep learning

A form of machine learning using neural networks with many layers. It powers much of modern vision, speech, and generative AI by learning complex representations from large datasets.

Policy lensDeep models can be highly capable but difficult to inspect, increasing the importance of evaluation, documentation, and external testing.

04

Core term

Neural network

A model made of connected computational units arranged in layers. Training changes the strengths of connections so the network maps inputs to useful outputs.

Policy lensThe biological metaphor can mislead. These systems are mathematical structures, not evidence of consciousness or human-like understanding.

05

Core term

Algorithm

A defined procedure for transforming inputs into outputs. In AI, algorithms may govern training, search, ranking, optimization, or decision-making.

Policy lens“The algorithm” is often shorthand for a much larger sociotechnical system that also includes data, objectives, interfaces, institutions, and people.

06

Core term

Model

A learned mathematical representation used to produce outputs from inputs. A model is usually one component inside a broader AI system that includes software, data flows, interfaces, and oversight.

Policy lensModel-level rules and system-level rules address different actors and risks. Do not treat the terms as interchangeable.

07

Core term

Parameters / weights

The numerical values adjusted during training. Together they encode patterns the model uses to transform inputs into outputs. Parameter count is one rough measure of model scale—not capability by itself.

Policy lensCompute, data quality, architecture, and training method can matter as much as raw parameter count when setting thresholds.

08

Core term

Dataset / training data

A collection of examples used to train, validate, or test a model. It may contain text, images, audio, code, measurements, labels, or records from many sources.

Policy lensKey questions include provenance, consent, copyright, privacy, representativeness, security, documentation, and who is missing from the data.

09

Lifecycle

Training

The process of adjusting a model’s parameters to improve performance on an objective. Training can include pretraining on broad data and later adaptation for specific tasks or behavior.

Policy lensTraining-stage controls address data, compute, security, labor, environmental impact, and capability development before deployment.

10

Lifecycle

Inference

Using a trained model to produce an output from a new input. For a chatbot, every generated response is an inference operation.

Policy lensInference-stage rules focus on access, use context, user disclosures, monitoring, incident response, and the consequences of outputs.

11

Training method

Supervised learning

Learning from examples paired with desired answers or labels. The model is trained to predict the correct output for new, similar inputs.

Policy lensLabels reflect human and institutional judgments. Their definitions, quality, and consistency can embed policy choices into a system.

12

Training method

Reinforcement learning (RL)

Learning behavior through feedback expressed as rewards or penalties. The system explores actions and updates its strategy to increase expected reward.

Policy lensThe reward function is a governance choice. Poorly specified rewards can produce shortcuts, gaming, or behavior that satisfies the metric but defeats the purpose.

13

Infrastructure

Compute

The processing resources used to train or run AI—including chips, memory, networking, electricity, and data-center capacity. Training compute is often estimated in floating-point operations (FLOPs).

Policy lensCompute is measurable and concentrated, making it a potential governance lever, but thresholds can become outdated or miss efficiency gains.

14

Model class

Foundation model

A model trained on broad data that can be adapted for many downstream tasks. The term covers more than chatbots and may include language, vision, audio, scientific, or multimodal models. NIST basis

Policy lensOne upstream model can affect many sectors, raising questions about shared responsibility across providers, deployers, and downstream users.

15

Moving category

Frontier model

Policy and industry shorthand for models at the leading edge of general-purpose capability. There is no single enduring technical threshold; what counts as frontier changes over time and across institutions.

Policy lensAny rule using this label needs measurable criteria, an update mechanism, and clarity on whether capability, compute, reach, or risk controls the designation.

02 · Generative systems

Models that create and act

The vocabulary behind systems that generate text, images, audio, video, and actions—and the techniques used to adapt, connect, and constrain them.

16

Model class

Generative AI

AI designed to produce new content such as text, code, images, audio, or video. Outputs are generated from learned statistical patterns rather than retrieved as fixed records.

Policy lensImportant issues include provenance, intellectual property, privacy, deception, labor impacts, harmful content, and accountability for downstream use.

17

Model class

Large language model (LLM)

A large model trained on sequences of language or code, usually to predict tokens. This enables generation, summarization, translation, classification, and other language tasks.

Policy lensFluent output is not proof of accuracy, reasoning, intent, or comprehension. Evaluate performance in the actual use context.

18

Architecture

Transformer

A neural-network architecture built around attention mechanisms that weigh relationships among parts of an input. Transformers underpin most modern LLMs and many multimodal models. Original paper

Policy lensArchitecture helps explain capability and cost, but policy outcomes also depend on data, scale, training, deployment, and access.

19

Model input

Token

A unit a model processes—often a word, part of a word, punctuation mark, or code fragment. Models count context and usage in tokens rather than pages or words.

Policy lensToken counts affect cost, context limits, compute estimates, and some legal thresholds; conversions to words vary by language and tokenizer.

20

Representation

Embedding

A numerical representation that places related items near one another in a mathematical space. Embeddings support search, clustering, recommendation, and retrieval.

Policy lensEmbeddings can encode sensitive attributes and social bias even when those fields are not explicitly included.

21

Model limit

Context window

The maximum amount of input and generated material a model can consider at one time. It is commonly measured in tokens and is not the same as persistent memory.

Policy lensLong context can enable document analysis while increasing privacy, security, attention, and data-retention concerns.

22

Model class

Multimodal model

A model that processes or produces more than one type of data, such as text, images, audio, or video, within one system.

Policy lensRisk assessment must cover cross-modal failures—for example, an image changing a text response or audio revealing biometric information.

23

Architecture

Diffusion model

A generative model that learns to reverse a gradual noising process. Starting from noise, it iteratively denoises toward an image, audio sample, video, or other output. Original paper

Policy lensDiffusion is a technical method, not a risk category. Governance usually focuses on training data, generated content, provenance, and use.

24

System pattern

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

A system that retrieves documents or records and gives them to a generative model as context. RAG can improve specificity and freshness without changing the model’s underlying parameters. Original paper

Policy lensRAG can support traceability, but it does not guarantee accuracy. Retrieval quality, permissions, citations, and source integrity still require controls.

25

Adaptation

Fine-tuning

Additional training that adjusts an existing model for a task, domain, style, or behavior. It can improve usefulness but may also add or remove safeguards.

Policy lensFine-tuning complicates responsibility: the base-model provider and downstream modifier may control different risks.

26

Interaction

Prompt / prompt engineering

A prompt is input that directs a model; prompt engineering is the practice of designing that input to improve relevance, format, reasoning steps, or safety.

Policy lensPrompting can improve outputs but is not a substitute for system controls, evaluation, access rules, or human accountability.

27

Evolving term

Agent / agentic AI

An AI system that can pursue a goal across multiple steps, often by planning, using tools, checking results, and taking actions with limited human intervention.

Policy lensAsk what actions are permitted, what approvals are required, how identity and authority are managed, and how the system can be stopped or audited.

28

Colloquial label

Hallucination / confabulation

Confidently presented content that is false, erroneous, internally inconsistent, or unsupported. NIST uses “confabulation”; “hallucination” is common industry shorthand. NIST basis

Policy lensRisk depends on context. A fictional image may be intended; a fabricated legal citation or medical fact can be consequential.

29

System quality

Grounding

Connecting a model’s output to specified evidence, data, tools, or the external world. Grounding can constrain generation and make claims easier to verify.

Policy lensAsk what sources count as ground truth, who controls them, how current they are, and whether the output actually cites what supports it.

30

Data practice

Synthetic data

Data generated or simulated rather than directly observed. It can augment scarce datasets, support testing, or reduce exposure of real records, but may reproduce model errors and bias.

Policy lens“Synthetic” does not automatically mean anonymous, unbiased, accurate, or free of rights and provenance concerns.

31

EU legal category

General-purpose AI (GPAI)

Under the EU AI Act, a GPAI model displays significant generality, performs a wide range of distinct tasks, and can be integrated into varied downstream systems. The legal text includes exclusions and additional duties. EU AI Act

Policy lensGPAI is not a synonym for AGI. It is a present-day legal category, not a claim of human-level general intelligence.

32

Access model

Open-weight vs. open-source AI

Open-weight models release learned parameters; open-source AI is a broader claim about freedom to use, study, modify, and share the system. Weights alone may omit training code, data information, and other components. OSI basis

Policy lensAsk exactly what is released, under which license, with what documentation, and whether restrictions apply. “Open” is not a binary technical fact.

33

Research concept

Scaling laws

Empirical relationships showing how model performance changes with inputs such as compute, data, and parameter count. They help forecast training tradeoffs within observed regimes.

Policy lensScaling laws are trends, not guarantees. Extrapolation may fail when architectures, data, tasks, or evaluation methods change.

34

Contested framing

Emergent capabilities

Abilities that appear or become measurable as models scale, sometimes seeming abrupt. Apparent emergence can reflect real nonlinear change, evaluation thresholds, or the way performance is measured.

Policy lensThe term should prompt better testing, not mystical claims. Ask whether the capability was truly absent, merely unmeasured, or hidden by the benchmark.

03 · Governance & safety

Evidence, oversight, and harm

Terms used to assess whether an AI system works as intended, for whom, under what conditions, and with what evidence and accountability.

35

Assessment

Evaluation (eval)

A structured assessment of a model or system against defined criteria. Evals may test capability, safety, security, bias, reliability, user outcomes, or real-world impact.

Policy lensAsk who designed the test, what population and conditions it covers, what threshold counts as acceptable, and whether results are reproducible.

36

Assessment

Benchmark

A standardized task or test suite used to compare performance. Benchmarks offer common reference points but can be narrow, saturated, contaminated, or disconnected from deployment conditions.

Policy lensA leaderboard score is not a complete risk assessment. Look for validity, data leakage controls, uncertainty, and real-world relevance.

37

Assessment

Red teaming

Structured testing designed to uncover flaws, vulnerabilities, harmful behavior, or misuse pathways, often by acting like an adversary. NIST basis

Policy lensRed teaming finds problems; it does not prove safety. Coverage, tester independence, remediation, and disclosure rules matter.

38

Trustworthiness

Robustness

The ability to maintain acceptable performance under changing, noisy, unexpected, or adversarial conditions. A robust system should fail predictably rather than collapse outside a narrow test setting.

Policy lensRobust to what? Requirements should name the relevant populations, environments, attacks, and failure modes.

39

Trustworthiness

Explainability / interpretability

Explainability concerns understandable reasons for outputs or behavior; interpretability concerns understanding how a model works. The terms overlap and are used inconsistently.

Policy lensThe necessary explanation depends on audience and decision: a developer, regulator, affected person, and court may need different evidence.

40

Safety concept

Alignment

Efforts to make an AI system’s goals and behavior consistent with intended human objectives, constraints, and values. This includes both specifying what is wanted and ensuring the system reliably follows it.

Policy lensAligned with whom, about what, and through which process? The term can hide disagreement over values, authority, and distributional effects.

41

Field

AI safety

The field concerned with preventing and reducing harm from AI systems. It can include reliability, misuse, security, human factors, systemic impacts, loss of control, and catastrophic risk.

Policy lensAlways ask which harms and time horizons are in scope. Different communities use “safety” more narrowly or broadly.

42

Field

AI governance

The rules, institutions, processes, and practices that shape AI development and use. Governance can be public or private, binding or voluntary, and can operate across the AI lifecycle.

Policy lensEffective governance allocates responsibility, creates evidence and oversight, supports enforcement, and adapts as capabilities and uses change.

43

Impact

Bias

A systematic tendency or difference in data, design, or outcomes. Some bias is statistical or intentional; harmful bias produces unjustified disadvantage, stereotyping, or discrimination.

Policy lens“Remove all bias” is not operational. Identify the affected groups, mechanism, baseline, harm, and legal or normative standard.

44

Impact

Fairness

A normative judgment about whether treatment, opportunities, burdens, or outcomes are just. Technical fairness metrics formalize different goals and can conflict.

Policy lensChoosing a metric is a policy decision, not only a technical one. It should connect to context, law, remedy, and affected communities.

45

Oversight

AI assurance

Processes that build justified confidence that an AI system meets specified requirements. Assurance can use testing, audit, documentation, impact assessment, verification, validation, and monitoring.

Policy lensAssurance is evidence, not a guarantee. Independence, competence, access, standards, scope, and continuous review determine its credibility.

04 · Future-facing concepts

Capability claims and long-term risk

Widely discussed terms without universally accepted tests or timelines. Use them carefully: each can bundle technical forecasts, values, and political assumptions.

46

No universal test

Artificial general intelligence (AGI)

A proposed level of AI with broad, flexible capability across many tasks and domains, often compared with human general competence. Organizations define AGI differently; no single test or threshold is accepted. Research basis

Policy lensAGI is not GPAI. Any policy trigger needs operational criteria for breadth, performance, autonomy, deployment, and risk—not the acronym alone.

47

Speculative category

Artificial superintelligence (ASI)

A hypothetical artificial system whose general cognitive capabilities greatly exceed those of individual humans—and, in some definitions, large human organizations. ASI is usually treated as beyond AGI. Research basis

Policy lensClaims about ASI should state the comparison group, domains, autonomy, access to resources, and evidence—not just “smarter than humans.”

48

Capability concept

Superintelligence

The general concept of intelligence that dramatically exceeds human cognitive performance across important domains. “ASI” specifies an artificial system; in practice the terms are often used interchangeably.

Policy lensThe governance problem is not a score alone. Deployment scale, autonomy, replication, strategic power, control, and concentration matter.

49

Hypothesis

Technological singularity

A hypothesis that machine superhuman intelligence could trigger rapid, self-reinforcing technological change that makes subsequent social and technical developments unusually difficult to forecast. Vinge essay

Policy lensThe singularity is not synonymous with AGI, nor is it a settled prediction or scheduled date. Separate scenario analysis from certainty.

50

Informal shorthand

p(doom)

An informal subjective estimate of the probability that advanced AI causes an existential or civilization-scale catastrophe. People use different outcomes, time horizons, and assumptions, so percentages are rarely directly comparable. CSET analysis

Policy lensAsk: probability of what, by when, through which pathway, under what governance, and based on what evidence? The reasoning matters more than the number.
